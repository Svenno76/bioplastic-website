The term "chemical recycling" has become a catch-all for any process that breaks plastic polymers down to molecular building blocks. But the technologies grouped under this label differ fundamentally in chemistry, engineering, economics, and readiness. For investors, policymakers, and corporate strategists, treating them as interchangeable leads to flawed capital allocation and unrealistic policy targets.

This analysis compares seven distinct approaches across the metrics that matter for commercial deployment: Technology Readiness Level (TRL), product yield, feedstock tolerance, capital and operating economics, energy intensity, output quality, and commercial footprint.

---

## The Seven Technologies at a Glance

| Technology | Core Chemistry | Primary Output | TRL (2026) | Key Developers |
|------------|----------------|----------------|------------|----------------|
| **Pyrolysis** | Thermal cracking (400–600°C, no O₂) | Pyrolysis oil (naphtha-range) | 8–9 | Alterra, Plastic Energy, Agilyx, Quantafuel |
| **Hydrothermal Liquefaction (HTL)** | Water at supercritical conditions (300–370°C, 100–250 bar) | Biocrude / synthetic crude | 6–7 | Licella (Cat-HTR), Mura Technology, KBR |
| **Solvolysis (Methanolysis / Glycolysis)** | Alcoholysis / glycolysis (180–280°C, catalysts) | Monomers (BHET, DMT, EG) | 7–8 | Eastman, Loop Industries, Ioniqa, Carbios (enzymatic variant) |
| **Enzymatic Recycling** | Enzyme-catalyzed depolymerization (50–70°C) | Monomers (TPA, EG) | 5–6 | Carbios, Samsara Eco, Protein Evolution |
| **Hydrochemolytic™ (HCT)** | Catalytic hydrotreatment with water (250–350°C, 30–70 bar) | Liquid hydrocarbons (naphtha/diesel) | 6–7 | Aduro Clean Technologies |
| **Gasification** | Partial oxidation (>700°C, sub-stoichiometric O₂) | Syngas (CO + H₂) | 8–9 | Enerkem, Fulcrum BioEnergy, Sierra Energy |
| **Solvent Dissolution** | Selective dissolution/precipitation (physical, not chemical) | Near-virgin polymer | 7–8 | PureCycle (PP), APK AG (PE), Polystyvert (PS) |

## 1. Technology Readiness Level (TRL) Assessment

**TRL 8–9 (Commercial / Near-Commercial): Pyrolysis, Gasification**

Pyrolysis has the deepest commercial footprint. Alterra's 60 kt/yr plant in the Netherlands (with Neste/Technip via Nerea), Plastic Energy's two 33 kt/yr plants in Spain, and Agilyx's 50 kt/yr facility in Oregon represent proven continuous operation. Gasification's syngas route is mature (Enerkem's 38 kt/yr Edmonton plant operating since 2014), but plastic-to-syngas is a smaller fraction of total capacity.

**TRL 7–8 (Demo / First Commercial): Solvolysis, Solvent Dissolution**

Eastman's 110 kt/yr methanolysis plant in Kingsport, TN (operational 2024) and Loop Industries' Infinite Loop™ technology (commercial partnership with Indorama) push solvolysis toward TRL 8. PureCycle's 49 kt/yr Ironton, OH facility (PP dissolution, licensed from P&G) reached mechanical completion in 2023; APK AG's Newcycling® (PE dissolution) operates at 8 kt/yr in Merseburg, Germany.

**TRL 6–7 (Pilot / Demo Scale): HTL, Hydrochemolytic™**

Licella's Cat-HTR™ has a 20 kt/yr demo in Australia and a 20 kt/yr commercial plant under construction at Wilton, UK (with Mura Technology). Aduro's Hydrochemolytic™ achieved 86% liquid yield on PP in its Next Generation Process (NGP) pilot; the FOAK plant at Chemelot (Netherlands) with Saipem for FEED is targeting 2027–2028 startup.

**TRL 5–6 (Lab / Pilot): Enzymatic**

Carbios' demonstration plant in Longlaville, France (operational 2023, 50 kt/yr nameplate for PET) is the furthest advanced. However, enzyme stability, reaction time (10–20 hours), and cost per kg of enzyme remain hurdles for polyolefin expansion.

## 2. Feedstock Tolerance & Preprocessing Requirements

| Technology | Accepted Polymers | Contaminant Tolerance | Preprocessing Complexity |
|------------|-------------------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| **Pyrolysis** | PE, PP, PS, mixed polyolefins | Moderate: metals, PVC (<1%), PET (<5%) cause corrosion/coking | Shredding, density separation, PVC removal, drying |
| **HTL** | All plastics (incl. PET, nylon), biomass co-feed | High: water handles organics; inorganics settle | Size reduction, feeding system for slurry |
| **Solvolysis** | PET (methanolysis/glycolysis), PA, PU, PLA | Low: requires >90% target polymer; colors, additives problematic | Intensive sorting, washing, decontamination |
| **Enzymatic** | PET (primary), emerging for PU, PLA | Very low: enzymes inhibited by additives, dyes, cross-contamination | Extensive washing, amorphous region exposure |
| **Hydrochemolytic™** | PE, PP, PS, mixed polyolefins, bitumen | High: water phase separates inorganics; catalysts tolerate heteroatoms | Moderate: size reduction, metal removal |
| **Gasification** | Any carbonaceous material (plastics, biomass, MSW) | Very high: tars, inorganics managed in slag | Drying, sizing, feeding system |
| **Solvent Dissolution** | PP (PureCycle), PE (APK), PS (Polystyvert) | Moderate: targets specific polymer; additives, fillers, other polymers must be removed | High: multi-step washing, solvent recovery, drying |

**Key Insight:** Feedstock preprocessing often represents 30–50% of total capex and is the hidden cost driver. Technologies accepting mixed, contaminated streams (pyrolysis, HTL, HCT, gasification) have a structural advantage over those requiring pure feedstocks (solvolysis, enzymatic, dissolution).

## 3. Product Yield & Output Quality

| Technology | Typical Yield (wt% of plastic in) | Primary Product | Quality / Drop-in Compatibility |
|------------|-----------------------------------|-----------------|----------------------------------|
| **Pyrolysis** | 65–80% (oil), 10–15% (gas), 5–10% (char) | Pyrolysis oil (naphtha-range) | Requires hydrotreating (H₂, catalyst) for steam cracker feed; variable contaminants (N, O, Cl, metals) |
| **HTL** | 60–75% (biocrude), 10–20% (gas), 5–10% (aqueous) | Biocrude (higher O-content than pyrolysis oil) | Needs upgrading (hydrodeoxygenation) for cracker feed; more oxygenates than pyrolysis oil |
| **Solvolysis (PET)** | 85–95% (monomer recovery) | BHET / DMT + EG | Monomer purity >99.5% achievable; direct repolymerization |
| **Enzymatic (PET)** | 90–97% (monomer recovery) | TPA + EG | High purity; enzyme residues require removal |
| **Hydrochemolytic™** | 80–88% (liquid hydrocarbon) | Naphtha/diesel-range hydrocarbons | Low heteroatoms (N, O, S <100 ppm); direct steam cracker feed claimed |
| **Gasification** | 70–85% (carbon to syngas) | Syngas (CO/H₂ ratio ~0.5–1.0) | Syngas cleanup critical (tars, H₂S, CO₂); feeds methanol/FT synthesis |
| **Solvent Dissolution** | 85–95% (polymer recovery) | Near-virgin polymer (PP, PE, PS) | Near-virgin quality; color/odor removal; direct conversion |

**Yield Nuance:** "Yield" definitions vary. Pyrolysis/HTL/HCT report *liquid yield* (including gases recycled). Solvolysis/enzymatic/dissolution report *monomer/polymer recovery*. Gasification reports *carbon conversion to syngas*. For circularity metrics, monomer/polymer recovery (solvolysis, enzymatic, dissolution) scores highest on material circularity; liquid hydrocarbon routes (pyrolysis, HTL, HCT) require cracker re-polymerization (additional yield loss ~15–20%).

## 4. Economics: Capex, Opex, and Green Premium

| Technology | Capex ($/t annual capacity) | Opex ($/t output, excl. feedstock) | Energy Intensity (MJ/kg output) | Green Premium vs. Fossil Naphtha |
|------------|------------------------------|-------------------------------------|----------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Pyrolysis** | $800–1,500 | $200–400 | 8–12 | +30–80% (highly feedstock/margin dependent) |
| **HTL** | $1,200–2,000 | $300–500 | 10–15 | +50–120% (high pressure, upgrading needed) |
| **Solvolysis (PET)** | $1,500–2,500 | $400–700 | 12–18 | +20–60% (monomer premium offsets) |
| **Enzymatic** | $2,000–3,500 | $500–1,000 | 15–25 | +80–200% (enzyme cost, slow kinetics) |
| **Hydrochemolytic™** | $1,000–1,800 | $250–450 | 6–10 | +20–50% (lower T/P, water-based, claimed) |
| **Gasification** | $2,500–4,000 | $400–800 | 20–30 | +100–200% (syngas cleanup, scale req.) |
| **Solvent Dissolution** | $1,800–3,000 | $500–900 | 10–20 | +40–100% (solvent recovery, near-virgin premium) |

**Capex Notes:** 
- Pyrolysis benefits from modular, skid-mounted designs (Nerea productization targeting <$1,000/t).
- Gasification requires massive scale (>100 kt/yr) for economic viability — high minimum efficient scale.
- Solvolysis/enzyme/dissolution capex elevated by corrosion-resistant alloys, solvent recovery trains, drying.

**Opex Drivers:**
- **Feedstock cost** (not in table) dominates: $200–500/t for mixed bales; negative cost possible with gate fees.
- **Hydrogen consumption:** Pyrolysis oil hydrotreating (2–4% H₂), HTL upgrading (4–6% H₂), HCT claims lower H₂ demand.
- **Solvent/enzyme make-up:** Dissolution loses 1–3% solvent/cycle; enzymes $5–20/kg at industrial scale (target <$1/kg).
- **Catalyst replacement:** Pyrolysis/HCT/HTL catalysts $50–200/kg, replaced every 6–24 months.

**Green Premium Reality Check:** Current pyrolysis oil trades at $800–1,200/t vs. fossil naphtha ~$650/t (2024 avg). The premium is *not* structural — it reflects scarcity of certified circular feedstock. As capacity scales (2027–2030), premium compresses toward processing cost differential (~$100–200/t).

## 5. Commercial Footprint & Strategic Positioning

### **Pyrolysis: The Incumbent Scaling Fast**
- **Nerea (Alterra + Neste + Technip):** Productized modular offering targeting <18-month deployment. 60 kt/yr reference plant.
- **Plastic Energy:** 2×33 kt/yr Spain; partnerships with TotalEnergies, ExxonMobil, SABIC.
- **Agilyx:** 50 kt/yr Oregon; JV with AmSty for PS depolymerization.
- **Strategic role:** Feedstock-flexible, cracker-integrated, policy-aligned (PPWR counts pyrolysis oil as recycled content).

### **HTL: The Water-Based Challenger**
- **Licella/Mura (Cat-HTR™):** Wilton 20 kt/yr (2025), Teesside 80 kt/yr planned. Supercritical water handles wet, mixed feeds.
- **KBR/INEOS:** Licensing Cat-HTR for chemical sites.
- **Strategic role:** Complements pyrolysis for wet/contaminated streams; co-feeds biomass for bio-attribution.

### **Solvolysis/Enzymatic: The Monomer Specialists (PET-First)**
- **Eastman:** Kingsport 110 kt/yr methanolysis; 2nd plant France (2027). Vertically integrated to specialty plastics.
- **Loop/Indorama:** Infinite Loop™ licensing model; Korea plant 2025.
- **Carbios:** Longlaville 50 kt/yr enzymatic; licensing to Indorama, Novozymes.
- **Strategic role:** Only route to *true* monomer circularity for condensation polymers (PET, PA, PU, PLA). PPWR recognizes depolymerization as "recycling."

### **Hydrochemolytic™: The Low-Temperature Polyolefin Route**
- **Aduro:** NGP pilot 86% yield on PP; Chemelot FOAK with Saipem FEED. Water-as-agent differentiator.
- **Strategic role:** Targets polyolefins at lower severity than pyrolysis; claims direct cracker feed without hydrotreating.

### **Gasification: The Syngas Platform Play**
- **Enerkem:** Edmonton 38 kt/yr (MSW); Rotterdam aviation fuel project with Shell.
- **Fulcrum:** Sierra BioFuels (US) — MSW to jet fuel via FT.
- **Strategic role:** Ultimate feedstock flexibility; produces platform molecules (methanol, FT liquids) not just naphtha.

### **Solvent Dissolution: The Physical Purification Benchmark**
- **PureCycle (PP):** Ironton 49 kt/yr; P&G technology. Near-virgin PP at ~$2,000/t premium.
- **APK AG (PE):** Newcycling® 8 kt/yr Merseburg; solvent-based PE purification.
- **Polystyvert (PS):** Dissolution/precipitation for polystyrene.
- **Strategic role:** Not chemical recycling (no bond cleavage). Produces polymer directly — highest material circularity. Limited to sortable, single-polymer streams.

## 6. Policy & Certification Alignment

| Technology | PPWR Recycled Content Eligibility | EU Taxonomy | ISCC Plus / RSB | Key Policy Risk |
|------------|-----------------------------------|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Pyrolysis** | ✅ Yes (mass balance) | ✅ Delegated Act | ✅ Mature | Mass balance rules tightening (fuel-use exemption phase-out) |
| **HTL** | ✅ Yes (mass balance) | ✅ Emerging | ✅ Emerging | Classification as "chemical recycling" vs. "advanced biofuel" |
| **Solvolysis** | ✅ Yes (full monomer) | ✅ | ✅ | Clearest eligibility — monomer = recycling |
| **Enzymatic** | ✅ Yes (full monomer) | ✅ | ⚠️ Emerging | Novelty = slower certification |
| **Hydrochemolytic™** | ✅ Yes (mass balance) | ⚠️ Pending | ⚠️ Emerging | New technology = certification lag |
| **Gasification** | ⚠️ Syngas route complex | ⚠️ Indirect | ✅ Syngas pathways | Fuel-use dominates; plastic-to-chemical allocation complex |
| **Solvent Dissolution** | ✅ Yes (physical recycling) | ✅ Mechanical equiv. | ✅ | Simplest compliance — polymer output = recycled content |

**Critical Policy Context:** The EU's Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) mandates 10–35% recycled content in plastic packaging by 2030. Mechanical recycling alone cannot meet this for food-contact polyolefins. Chemical recycling (pyrolysis, HTL, HCT, solvolysis) via mass balance is the *only* compliant pathway — creating a structural demand pull. However, the European Commission's delegated acts on mass balance methodology (expected 2025–2026) will determine whether "fuel-use" allocation dilutes the incentive.

## 7. Strategic Implications by Stakeholder

### **For Investors**
- **Near-term (2024–2027):** Pyrolysis modular deployments (Nerea-type) offer lowest risk, fastest revenue. Solvent dissolution (PureCycle/APK) offers premium polymer margins but feedstock risk.
- **Medium-term (2027–2030):** HTL and HCT FOAK plants de-risk; watch Chemelot (Aduro) and Wilton (Mura) startup data. Solvolysis capacity doubles (Eastman 2nd plant, Loop Korea).
- **Long-term (2030+):** Enzymatic for polyolefins? Gasification for platform chemicals? Winner-take-most dynamics in each technology lane.

### **For Brand Owners (Packaging, Automotive, Textiles)**
- **PET/Polyester:** Solvolysis/enzymatic offer true bottle-to-bottle circularity. Secure offtake now (Eastman, Loop, Carbios capacity sold out through 2028).
- **Polyolefins (PE/PP):** Pyrolysis oil via mass balance is the *only* scalable food-contact route today. Secure allocations with cracker-integrated players (Neste, Borealis, SABIC, TotalEnergies).
- **Mixed/Complex Waste:** HTL and HCT emerging for multilayers, films, contaminated streams. Early partnerships de-risk future supply.

### **For Policymakers**
- **Mass balance rules** must prevent fuel-use loophole without killing investment case. Fuel-exempt allocation should phase down 2025–2030.
- **Certification harmonization** across ISCC Plus, RSB, national schemes to avoid fragmentation.
- **Feedstock access:** Waste shipment rules, end-of-waste criteria, and sorting mandates determine whether capacity can be fed.

### **For Technology Developers**
- **Productization** (Nerea model) beats bespoke engineering — standardize, modularize, pre-fabricate.
- **Integration** > standalone: co-locate with crackers (pyrolysis/HCT), refineries (HTL), or monomer plants (solvolysis).
- **Feedstock contracts** are as important as technology — secure 10+ year supply with municipalities/sorters.

## 9. Conclusions

1. **"Chemical recycling" is not a monolith.** Seven distinct technologies serve different polymer families, waste streams, and economic models.
2. **Pyrolysis leads on deployment** but faces margin compression and mass balance policy risk.
3. **Solvolysis/enzymatic own the condensation polymer space** (PET, PA, PU) — true circularity, premium pricing.
4. **HCT and HTL are the polyolefin challengers** — lower severity, water-based, but must prove FOAK economics in 2027–2028.
5. **Solvent dissolution is physical, not chemical** — but delivers highest material circularity for sortable streams.
6. **Policy is the market maker.** PPWR recycled content mandates create the demand pull; mass balance rules determine the economics.
7. **Integration wins.** Standalone plants face higher capex, feedstock risk, and offtake uncertainty. Cluster-integrated (Chemelot, Porvoo, Wilton, Chemelot) is the model.
