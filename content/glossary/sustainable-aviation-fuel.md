**Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF)** is aviation turbine fuel produced from renewable or waste-based feedstocks rather than fossil crude oil. It is chemically similar — in the certified "drop-in" pathways, essentially identical — to conventional jet fuel (Jet A/A-1), so it can be blended and used in existing aircraft and airport infrastructure without modification.

## Production Pathways

- **HEFA (Hydroprocessed Esters and Fatty Acids)** — hydroprocessing of vegetable oils, used cooking oil, and animal fats. The most mature and today's dominant route; [Neste](/companies/neste/) produces HEFA-based SAF at scale using its proprietary NExBTL technology (see [NExBTL](/glossary/nexbtl/)).
- **ATJ (Alcohol-to-Jet)** — fermentation of sugars or cellulosic biomass to ethanol or isobutanol, followed by catalytic dehydration and oligomerization to jet-range hydrocarbons.
- **PtL (Power-to-Liquid)** — synthesis of hydrocarbons from green hydrogen and captured CO2 via Fischer-Tropsch; the most scalable long-term route but not yet commercial.
- **Other pathways** — municipal solid waste gasification, pyrolysis oils, and direct sugar-to-hydrocarbon processes are in various stages of certification.

## Feedstocks and Competition

HEFA SAF depends on waste lipid feedstocks — used cooking oil foremost among them — which are limited in supply and shared with renewable diesel production. ATJ and PtL routes draw on sugars, lignocellulosic biomass, and renewable electricity, connecting SAF to the same renewable carbon feedstock pools the bioplastics industry relies on; feedstock competition between fuels and materials is an ongoing industry debate.

## Regulation

The EU's **ReFuelEU Aviation** regulation mandates blending shares of SAF at EU airports — 2% from 2025, rising to 6% in 2030 and 70% by 2050 — creating a large guaranteed demand pull, while the US relies on tax credits (the 45Z clean fuel production credit) and airline offtake agreements.

## Relevance to Bioplastics

SAF illustrates the broader renewable carbon economy in which bioplastics operate: both sectors compete for waste oils, residues, and green hydrogen, and both face the same certification logic (lifecycle GHG reduction versus fossil reference, certified under schemes such as ISCC).
