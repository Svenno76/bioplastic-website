**Liquefied waste plastic (LWP)** is the liquid intermediate produced when post-consumer plastic waste is thermochemically converted — typically by **pyrolysis** (thermal cracking at 400–800 °C without oxygen) or **hydrothermal liquefaction** (conversion in hot, pressurized water). It is the crucial intermediate on the path from mixed plastic waste back to virgin-quality plastics.

## Composition and Quality

LWP is a complex mixture of hydrocarbons whose composition depends on the input waste and the process conditions:

- **From polyolefins (PE, PP)** — a waxy-to-liquid naphtha-range oil rich in alkenes, paraffins, and aromatics, similar in boiling range to crude-derived naphtha.
- **Contaminants** — chlorine from PVC residues, nitrogen from polyamides, oxygen from polyesters, and metals from additives are the main quality issues; they must be removed before the oil can enter downstream petrochemistry.

## Refining into New Plastics

Because raw pyrolysis oil rarely meets steam-cracker feed specifications, it typically undergoes:

1. **Filtration and stabilization** — removal of solids and reactive species.
2. **Hydrotreatment** — catalytic removal of chlorine, nitrogen, and oxygen, and saturation of unstable olefins.
3. **Use as cracker feedstock** — the purified oil is co-fed into steam crackers, where it is cracked into ethylene and propylene for new polymer production.

## Role in the Circular Economy

LWP is the pivot of **chemical recycling**: it allows plastic waste that mechanical recycling cannot handle (mixed, multi-layer, contaminated streams) to substitute fossil naphtha in the production of food-contact-grade polymers. Certification schemes such as **ISCC PLUS** use **mass balance** to allocate the recycled content of LWP to specific end products, which is how brand owners can claim recycled content from cracker-scale operations.
