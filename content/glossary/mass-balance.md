The **mass balance approach** is a chain-of-custody model used to track certified sustainable inputs (bio-based or recycled feedstocks) through production systems where sustainable and conventional materials are physically mixed. It is the dominant certification mechanism for chemical recycling and bio-based chemicals produced in large shared plants.

## How It Works

In a mass balance system, certified sustainable feedstock enters a plant — for example, pyrolysis oil from plastic waste or bio-naphtha from vegetable oils — and is mixed with fossil feedstock in shared crackers and polymerization units. The physical mixing is not tracked molecule-by-molecule. Instead:

1. Certified inputs are recorded in **quantities** at the plant gate.
2. The plant may attribute those quantities to specific products leaving the gate, as long as the total attributed amount never exceeds the certified input (minus defined conversion losses).
3. Auditors certify the bookkeeping; the products carrying the claim can be marketed as "recycled-content" or "bio-based," even though the specific molecules may have come from fossil feed.

## Why It Matters

Mass balance makes sustainable chemistry economically possible at industrial scale. A steam cracker or a polymer plant cannot be run on a single feedstock and switched for each order, so bookkeeping-based attribution is the only practical way to sell certified sustainable output from existing assets. It underpins:

- **Chemical recycling claims** — plastic-waste pyrolysis oil entering crackers, with recycled content allocated to food-contact packaging.
- **Bio-based polymers** — bio-naphtha or bio-based monomers feeding shared plants.
- **Certification schemes** — ISCC PLUS, RSB, and REDcert all offer mass balance chain-of-custody modules.

## The Controversy

Critics — notably NGOs and the Zero Waste Europe network — argue that mass balance allows fossil plastics to be marketed as recycled when no physical recycled molecules may be in the specific product. The share of certified input that can be attributed per tonne of output (the "allocation percentage", sometimes 100%, sometimes lower) and the treatment of process losses are the main points of dispute, and the EU is still harmonizing the rules for chemical-recycling content accounting under the PPWR.
