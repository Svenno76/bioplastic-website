## Overview

A **biorefinery** is an integrated facility that converts biomass into a spectrum of marketable products — fuels, chemicals, materials, heat, and power — through a combination of biochemical and thermochemical processes. The concept is explicitly modeled on the petroleum refinery: rather than burning biomass for a single low-value output, a biorefinery **fractionates** the feedstock into its constituent components (cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, sugars, oils, proteins) and converts each into the highest-value product stream it can support.

## Types of Biorefineries

- **Sugar/starch platforms ("conventional")** — corn, sugarcane, wheat, or potato starch is fermented to ethanol, lactic acid, or other building blocks; co-products include animal feed (DDGS) and CO2. Examples: grain ethanol plants in the US Midwest, cane biorefineries in Brazil.
- **Lignocellulosic (2nd-generation) biorefineries** — agricultural residues, forestry biomass, or energy crops are pre-treated and enzymatically hydrolyzed into fermentable sugars, with lignin recovered as a fuel or a chemical feedstock.
- **Oleaginous biorefineries** — vegetable oils, used cooking oils, and animal fats are hydroprocessed into renewable diesel and sustainable aviation fuel (for example via [Neste](/companies/neste/)'s NExBTL technology, see [NExBTL](/glossary/nexbtl/)).
- **Thermochemical biorefineries** — gasification or [pyrolysis](/glossary/pyrolysis/) converts biomass into syngas or bio-oil, which is then upgraded to fuels and chemicals.
- **Marine/algae biorefineries** — microalgae or seaweed fractionated into lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates (an emerging category).

## Core Process Steps

1. **Feedstock logistics** — harvesting, collection, storage, and pre-treatment of biomass.
2. **Fractionation** — mechanical, chemical, or enzymatic separation of the biomass components.
3. **Conversion** — fermentation, enzymatic catalysis, gasification, pyrolysis, or hydroprocessing.
4. **Upgrading and separation** — purification of intermediates into fuel-, polymer-, or chemical-grade products.
5. **Energy integration** — using process residues (notably lignin) for combined heat and power, which is what makes many biorefineries energy self-sufficient.

## Industry Landscape

Europe's forest industry has been the most active biorefinery builder: **UPM** (with its biochemicals business converting paper-industry side streams into glycols and other molecules), **Stora Enso**, and **Borregaard** (the world's most advanced lignin-based biorefinery in Sarpsborg, Norway) all operate integrated facilities. **Sekab** develops cellulosic ethanol technology in Sweden; **Clariant** commercialized the sunliquid® cellulosic ethanol process; enzyme and biotech suppliers **Novozymes** and **DSM** underpin the biochemical routes; and agricultural processors **Roquette** and **Tereos** run large starch- and sugar-based platforms whose outputs include bioplastic building blocks such as lactic acid and sorbitol.

## Applications

- **Biofuels** — ethanol, renewable diesel, SAF (see [sustainable aviation fuel](/glossary/sustainable-aviation-fuel/))
- **Polymer building blocks** — lactic acid (for PLA), FDCA, succinic acid, glycols
- **Lignin valorization** — phenolic resins, carbon fibers, binders
- **Hemicellulose derivatives** — xylitol, furfural, dissolving pulp
- **Bio-based chemicals and materials** — solvents, surfactants, biodegradable films

## Standards & Sustainability

- **ISO 13065** — sustainability criteria for bioenergy, applied to biorefinery supply chains
- **RSB (Roundtable on Sustainable Biomaterials)** — the most widely referenced certification for sustainable feedstocks and chain of custody
- **ISCC PLUS** — certification of bio-based and circular content through mass balance in shared production assets (see [mass balance approach](/glossary/mass-balance-approach/))

## Significance

Biorefineries are the industrial backbone of the bio-based economy: they are where renewable feedstocks become the monomers, intermediates, and energy carriers that bioplastics, biochemicals, and biofuels are built from. Their economics hinge on **product diversification** (multiple revenue streams from one feedstock) and **feedstock flexibility**, and their sustainability credentials hinge on certified responsible sourcing of the biomass they consume.
