The European Bioplastics association (EUBP) has published a position paper arguing that certified biodegradable plastics — where their use is backed by standards and certification — belong at the centre of the EU's response to microplastic pollution rather than at its margins.

## The argument

The paper's case rests on a distinction between two kinds of microplastic risk: particles that persist in the environment indefinitely, and particles that biodegrade into biomass, water, and CO₂ within defined timeframes. Where a product's failure mode involves environmental leakage — agricultural films, fisheries gear, cosmetics, or packaging formats prone to litter — the association argues that specifying certified biodegradable polymers removes the persistence problem rather than merely relocating it.

## A test for the EU's microplastics rules

The intervention targets a live policy file: the European Commission's restriction on intentionally added microplastics. Biodegradable grades have sometimes been swept into restrictions drafted for persistent polymers, and the paper presses for certification-based differentiation — a European or ISO biodegradation certificate as the criterion for exemptions, rather than polymer class alone.

For bioplastics producers, the paper's significance is commercial as much as regulatory: European sales of biodegradable grades concentrate in exactly the applications — agricultural mulch films, compostable bags, food-service ware — where the persistence argument carries the most weight. A restriction regime that recognises certified biodegradation widens the addressable market; one that does not has the opposite effect.
