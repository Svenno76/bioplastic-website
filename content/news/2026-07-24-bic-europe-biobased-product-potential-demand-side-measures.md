The Bio-based Industries Consortium (BIC) has published a landmark study arguing that Europe possesses the industrial capacity and technical expertise to become a global leader in bio-based products — but only if policymakers implement targeted demand-side measures to unlock that potential. Released in July 2026, the study arrives at a critical moment as European legislators draft the next phase of the EU's bioeconomy strategy.

The research, produced in two parts, first maps the lead markets for bio-based products across four strategic sectors: chemicals, plastics and polymers, fibre-based packaging, and textiles. Each sector represents a significant share of European industrial output and offers a clear pathway to reduce fossil dependency. The second part translates these market insights into concrete policy recommendations, ranging from public procurement mandates and carbon pricing adjustments to consumer labelling schemes and investment de-risking instruments.

Philippe Mengal, Executive Director of BIC, emphasised the urgency: "The deployment of the bio-based industries in Europe can be a real competitive advantage, and strengthen our strategic autonomy at the same time. That potential stays untapped, without the market signals to unlock it. The legislation to do this is being written now, and we are ready to work with policymakers to get these measures into it."

The study highlights that Europe's bio-based industries already contribute substantially to the economy, yet their growth remains constrained by a persistent price gap with fossil-based alternatives. Current policy frameworks focus predominantly on supply-side support — research funding, pilot plants, and production incentives — while the demand side remains under-addressed. BIC argues that without parallel demand-pull mechanisms, even mature technologies struggle to reach commercial scale.

Among the recommended measures, the consortium proposes mandatory bio-based content targets for public procurement, a carbon border adjustment mechanism that accounts for the full lifecycle emissions of materials, and a harmonised European labelling system that enables consumers to identify and choose bio-based products. The report also calls for the recently announced Bio-based Europe Alliance (BEA), which aims to pool €10 billion in collective demand commitments from major brands and retailers, to be formalised through binding procurement pledges.

The findings resonate with broader EU policy developments. The European Commission's updated bioeconomy strategy, expected later in 2026, is widely anticipated to incorporate demand-side instruments for the first time. Several member states, including France, Germany, and the Netherlands, have already introduced national bio-based content requirements in construction and packaging — creating a patchwork that BIC argues should be harmonised at EU level.

Industry stakeholders have welcomed the study's evidence-based approach. Representatives from the chemical, packaging, and textile sectors noted that the lead market mapping provides a shared reference point for cross-value-chain collaboration. The textiles chapter, in particular, identifies bio-based polyester and polyamide as near-term opportunities, given existing recycling infrastructure and brand sustainability commitments.

The BIC study was developed with input from over 150 industry experts, academic researchers, and policy advisers. Its methodology combines techno-economic analysis of production pathways with market modelling under different policy scenarios. The full report is available on the BIC website and will be presented to the European Parliament's Committee on Industry, Research and Energy (ITRE) in September 2026.
